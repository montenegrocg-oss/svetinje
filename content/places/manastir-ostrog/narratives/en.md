Ostrog Monastery stands in the Ostrog cliffs, high above the Bjelopavlići Plain between Danilovgrad and Nikšić. The complex consists of the Upper and Lower Monasteries, created in different periods. Its history is inseparably linked with Saint Basil of Ostrog, whose relics are kept in the Upper Monastery.

Monastic life existed in the Ostrog caves before Saint Basil’s arrival. In the early seventeenth century, Venerable Isaiah of Ostrog lived ascetically in these cliffs with other hermits about whom little historical information survives. The Upper Monastery later developed on the site of their cave dwellings.

Saint Basil came to Ostrog in the mid-seventeenth century after serving at Tvrdoš Monastery and living near the contemporary town of Onogošt. He gathered monks in the Ostrog cliffs and began restoring and enlarging the existing community. He renewed the cave Church of the Entry of the Theotokos into the Temple and built the Church of the Precious and Life-Giving Cross, laying the foundations of the Upper Monastery as it developed over subsequent centuries.

Saint Basil of Ostrog reposed at Ostrog on 12 May 1671. He was buried near the Church of the Entry of the Theotokos, and seven years later his relics were taken from the grave and placed in a reliquary in the cave church. Ostrog gradually became one of the most important pilgrimage sites in the Serbian Orthodox Church.

The monastery suffered repeatedly in Ottoman campaigns during the eighteenth and nineteenth centuries. The attack during the war of 1852–1853 is particularly remembered: Turkish forces occupied the Lower Monastery, while defenders under Duke Mirko Petrović withdrew to Upper Ostrog and resisted from there. The monastery was plundered again in 1877, when Saint Basil’s relics were temporarily removed for safekeeping and returned after the war.

The Lower Monastery developed on land acquired by Saint Basil for the Ostrog community. Its central Church of the Holy Trinity was built in the early nineteenth century through the efforts of Saint Peter of Cetinje and Archimandrite Josif Pavićević of Ostrog. Residences and other monastery buildings were later built and restored around it.

A major fire struck Upper Ostrog on 1 June 1923. The cave churches and Saint Basil’s relics survived, but much of the monastery buildings was destroyed. Reconstruction began that year, and the new Upper Monastery complex was built to a design by the Russian architect Vladimir Sukurenko. Consecrated on 2 November 1924, it acquired much of the recognisable appearance preserved today.

Upper Ostrog, almost incorporated into a sheer cliff, is the monastery’s defining feature. It contains the cave Church of the Entry of the Theotokos, where Saint Basil’s relics rest, and the Church of the Precious Cross. The Lower Monastery, with its Holy Trinity Church, forms the second part of the same complex and developed into an important centre of monastic life and hospitality for pilgrims.
